Re-Entry Reading Guide

Learn how spacecraft slow down, manage heat, and descend safely through the atmosphere with clear visual ideas before running flight labs.

01 · The Basics

What happens during re-entry?

A spacecraft in orbit moves fast. Coming back safely means trading that high speed into controlled drag and heat protection as it meets the upper air.

01 · High Speed

Orbital velocity is huge

Before descending, a spacecraft carries massive energy from moving sideways around Earth—not just from altitude alone.

02 · Air Drag & Heat

Air slows the craft and warms the shield

As thin upper air compresses in front of the craft, drag slows it down while heat builds up along the entry face.

03 · Entry Angle

Too shallow or too steep matters

A balanced entry corridor keeps heating and deceleration manageable instead of bouncing off or diving in too hard.

02 · Heat Shields & Braking

How heat shields protect the craft

Most of a spacecraft's energy is turned into heat in the upper atmosphere. A heat shield keeps that heat away from the cabin while air drag does the heavy braking.

Focused Concept · 01

A wide, blunt face pushes hot air ahead

Instead of slicing sharply like a dart, a rounded heat shield creates a shock layer in front of the craft so much of the heat stays in the air rather than pouring straight into the hull.

Energy trade-off

Speed drops step by step as air resistance converts motion into heat and sound.

Safe inner cabin

Insulation and ablative layers protect the structure until speeds are low enough for final descent.

04 · Trade-Offs & Pitfalls

Avoid common entry mistakes

Small changes in angle, speed, or timing can turn a smooth descent into a tough recovery. Here is how each trade-off plays out.

Too Steep

Diving straight into dense air

A sharp plunge slows the craft too fast, causing intense G-loads and high peak heating in a short time.

Better Approach

Use a gradual entry corridor

Entering at a measured angle lets upper air brake the spacecraft steadily while keeping stress and heat within limits.

Too Shallow

Skipping off the top of the air

If the entry angle is too flat, the spacecraft can bounce back toward space instead of capturing into a landing path.

Better Approach

Confirm de-orbit burn precision

A clean de-orbit burn lowers perigee low enough into the atmosphere so drag holds the craft on a reliable descent.

Too Early

Opening chutes at high speed

Deploying parachutes while moving too fast creates extreme fabric loads and can tear the canopy.

Better Approach

Wait for safe subsonic speed

Let air drag and drogue stages bring speed down first so main parachutes open smoothly and safely.

05 · Heat Shield Metal

Metals and thermal materials

Strong metals give heat shields structural shape and load strength, while specialized thermal layers protect against extreme heat.

Alloy Profile · 01

Titanium Alloy Structures

Titanium alloys combine high strength with lower weight than many heavier metals and resist corrosion well. On their own, bare metals still need thermal tiles or shielding for severe orbital re-entry heat.

Primary Role Light Strong Frame
Heat Behavior Needs Thermal Tiles
Key Advantage High Strength-to-Weight
06 · Target Planet & Re-Entries

Every world changes how a craft lands

Atmosphere density, gravity, and entry speed vary across planets and moons. Compare four destinations to see how engineers adapt re-entry.

Destination Profile · Earth

Dense air slows capsules reliably before splashdown

Earth's nitrogen-oxygen atmosphere is thick enough to remove almost all orbital speed through drag and parachutes, making ocean splashdowns and cushioned land recoveries dependable.

Atmosphere 1.0 bar · Dense
Entry Speed ~7.8–11 km/s
Main Challenge High Peak Heating
Landing Method Heat Shield + Chutes
07 · Flight Path Angle (°)

Entry angle sets the descent path

The flight path angle is the slope between the craft's motion vector and the local horizontal. Slide the angle to see how a shallow or steep entry behaves.

-2.0° Shallow Entry

Longer air path, risk of skip-out

A small downward angle takes a long curve through thin upper air. If it is too shallow, aerodynamic lift and orbital speed can skip the craft back out toward space.

Heating Rate Gradual / Extended
G-Load Profile Low to Moderate
08 · Velocity

How speed drops from orbit to landing

Velocity is both speed and direction. Re-entry is all about shedding huge horizontal speed safely before reaching the surface.

High sideways speed keeps a craft in orbit

In low Earth orbit, moving horizontally around 7.8 km/s lets Earth curve away beneath the craft as fast as gravity pulls it down. A small retro-burn lowers the path into air.
